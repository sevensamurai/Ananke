# Caring for Your New Pet

## The First 48 Hours

The first two days are all about decompression. Your new pet has been through a lot — a shelter environment, car rides, new smells, and unfamiliar faces. Here's how to make the transition smoother.

For dogs, keep things low-key. Set up a quiet space with a bed, water bowl, and a chew toy. Let them explore at their own pace. Avoid overwhelming them with visitors or trips to the dog park right away. Many shelter dogs are overtired and overstimulated — they need boring more than they need fun.

For cats, prepare a single "base camp" room with a litter box, food, water, and a hiding spot (a cardboard box works great). Keep the door closed and let them emerge on their own schedule. Some cats take an hour to come out; others take a week. Both are normal.

## Feeding Schedule

Stick to a consistent feeding schedule rather than free-feeding. This helps with house training (for dogs) and lets you monitor appetite, which is one of the first indicators of health issues.

Puppies under 6 months: three meals per day. Adult dogs: two meals per day, morning and evening. Cats: two meals per day, or use a puzzle feeder for enrichment. We send every pet home with a sample of the food they've been eating at the shelter. If you want to switch brands, do it gradually over 7–10 days by mixing increasing amounts of the new food.

## Veterinary Care

Schedule a vet checkup within the first two weeks, even though your pet arrives vaccinated and spayed/neutered. This establishes a baseline health record with your own vet.

Core vaccinations are up to date at adoption, but your vet will set up a booster schedule. Heartworm prevention (for dogs) and flea/tick prevention (for all pets) should start immediately if not already active.

## House Training

For puppies and some adult dogs, expect accidents. Take them outside first thing in the morning, after meals, after naps, and before bedtime. Praise heavily when they go outside. Never punish accidents — just clean with an enzymatic cleaner and move on.

For cats, most shelter cats are already litter-trained. If your cat avoids the litter box, try a different litter type, add a second box, or move the box to a quieter location.

## Socialization & Enrichment

Dogs need regular walks (at least 30 minutes twice a day for most breeds) and mental stimulation. Puzzle feeders, training sessions, and sniff walks are all great enrichment.

Cats need vertical space (shelves, cat trees), scratching posts, and interactive play (wand toys, laser pointers). Even indoor-only cats benefit from "cat TV" — a window perch near a bird feeder.

Rabbits need at least 3–4 hours of supervised free-roam time daily. They can be litter-trained and love to explore.

## Common Behavioral Issues

Separation anxiety is common in newly adopted dogs. Build up alone time gradually — leave for 5 minutes, then 15, then 30. Leave a Kong stuffed with peanut butter to create positive associations.

Furniture scratching in cats is normal behavior, not misbehavior. Provide scratching posts near the furniture they're targeting and reward them when they use it.

Resource guarding (growling over food or toys) should be managed early with a positive-reinforcement trainer, not punished. Our behavior helpline can refer you to affordable trainers in your area.
